# Of The Green Cradle

*Excerpt from **The Crownweave Compendium**, Fourth Roadwarden’s Edition*  
 *Shelfmark: TV-IX-001, “On the Fast Country and Its Harvests”*

---

### **Of Iximvael, the Green Cradle**

A traveller on the White Road sees the Green Cradle long before entering it, and most never enter it at all. Somewhere past the second Stone, coming up from the sea, the forest on either side of the stone falls back, and the traveller finds that they are walking along the floor of a country of steps: hillside after hillside cut into terraces and planted to the skyline, green, then gold, then green again. If they stop at a strand-gate and watch for an hour, they will see it move. A terrace sown at breakfast shows green by the next day’s noon. A hillside that was gold when they passed it going up is bare and being sown when they pass it coming down.

The district is **Iximvael**, the maize-field, and the rest of the realm calls it **the Green Cradle**, because it raises Thal’vireth’s food and most of its children. It keeps the fastest pace that anyone in the realm will farm, some six lived years to every outside one, and it spends them on the one thing a fast country is good for: growing. The terraces fill the land on both sides of the White Road from the Tower’s eastern roots almost to the sea, from the Deep Count’s border in the north to the Quickening Wards’ slopes in the south, and the Road’s Strands run out into them like roots into soil.

If Sakbeleth is where Thal’vireth lets the world in, **Iximvael is what keeps all of it fed: the realm’s granary, its nursery and its busiest graveyard, all on the same hillside.**

---

### **I. The Temper of the Cradle**

Iximvael is a district of **short lives, full baskets, and no patience whatsoever**.

Its people know their pace the way people elsewhere know the weather, without thinking about it and without ever quite forgetting it. A Cradle farmer is born, grows, marries, raises children, works sixty lived years on a terrace and is planted, in about the time it takes an outside merchant to make a modest fortune. A Cradle family can name eleven generations within a single outside century, and most can recite them. From the Road, the Cradle looks like a country in a hurry. From inside it, the Cradle will tell you, it is everybody else who is standing still.

***The Slow Sun.*** The tree hurries the Cradle. It does not hurry the sky. The sun crosses Iximvael at the outside pace, once in every outside day, which is to say once in every six of the Cradle’s own. The Cradle therefore keeps two kinds of day: a **sun**, from one sunrise to the next, and a **sleep**, the day a body actually lives, and a sun is six sleeps, roughly three of light and three of dark. Cradle-folk live by sleeps. They work, eat and sleep under whatever part of the sky happens to be overhead, and they work the long nights by lamplight: the Cradle buys more of the Quickening Wards’ glyph-lamps than the rest of the realm together, and on a dark night the terraces are strung with them from the valley floor to the skyline, so that from the Road the hills seem to be holding a festival that never ends. Foreigners find the long day the hardest thing about the Cradle. Cradle-folk who go up to the Road find the short one worse, and complain that the sun there swings across the sky *like a lamp on a cart*.

***The Long Wet and the Long Dry.*** The seasons come at the outside pace as well. The rains that fall on the forest for half an outside year fall on the Cradle for three of its own years, and the dry season that follows lasts three more. A Cradle child may learn to walk, and to swim in the flooded lower terraces, before they have ever seen the rains end. The terraces were cut for this. Every step holds water, every hillside has its **chultun**, bottle-shaped cisterns cut into the rock beneath the terraces, filled through the Long Wet and drawn down, a jar at a time, through the Long Dry. A Cradle family’s wealth is reckoned in terraces and in cisterns, in that order, and a family that has lost its cistern has lost its terraces too, about two years later.

***The Field and the Tree.*** The Daykeepers teach that the tree spends its memory unevenly, and that the Cradle is where it spends most generously: here the present is made to hurry, because here the tree is pouring the past out fastest. The Cradle draws the obvious conclusion. If the tree gives most here, it must be fed most here, and the Cradle feeds it in the only coin the tree accepts. Funerals in the Cradle are more frequent than anywhere in the realm, and nobody in the Cradle thinks this is sad. *The tree eats first*, the Cradle says, at harvest, at weddings, at plantings, and at any meal at which a child has reached for the bread before their grandmother; and at every harvest the first ear cut from every terrace is carried to the root-bed and planted with the dead.

The Cradle calls the White Road **the Stone** and the slow districts **the Still**. To *go up the Stone* is to leave home, and is said of the young with a shrug and of the old with a certain look. To *go to the Still* is to go somewhere you will not come back from in time, and is not said of anyone kindly.

### **II. Those Who Work It**

Iximvael is the most populous district in the realm, at about forty thousand by the Ledger, and by far the youngest. It is also, by every other measure, the most elven: the Cradle’s life is hard to join from outside, and harder to leave.

**The terrace-lines** are the Cradle. Each terrace on each hillside is held by one family line, and the line’s name is cut into the terrace’s retaining wall, a fresh name with each new head of the household. A Cradle household is large and crowded, because three or four generations live under one roof as a matter of course: a Cradle grandmother is often not long past fifty lived years, and is expected to work. The lines intermarry across a hillside and quarrel across it with equal energy, and each hillside settles its own disputes in a **terrace moot**, a gathering of every line’s head on the uppermost step, which meets every sun, talks for most of it and is very hard to stop.

**The strand-folk** live where the Strands come in from the Road, in villages that keep the Cradle’s pace and half the Road’s manners. They are the Cradle’s traders, its carters and its matchmakers with the outside: more half-elves live in the strand villages than anywhere in the realm but the Road itself, and many of them are the grandchildren of Road-folk who stepped through a strand-gate to marry, and found their children grown before their parents on the Road had finished disapproving.

**The Granary carters** drive the Cradle’s harvest down to the sea, and are the only people in Thal’vireth who make a living out of the Cradle’s pace instead of merely living at it. The Granary Road runs through the Cradle and so keeps the Cradle’s time: a cart that leaves the Threshing Mounds reaches the White Road, four days’ drive away, in well under one outside day, and the harvest is on the quays at Nabireth while the Road’s own carters are still loading theirs. A carter’s Count-beads go green very quickly. Most carters drive for ten lived years, buy a terrace, and never go near a cart again.

**The Cradle-runners** are the carters’ wilder cousins. A message carried across the Cradle reaches the far side in a sixth of the outside time it would take on the Road, at the cost of the runner’s own days, and there are people on the Road and in the Exchange Houses who will pay very well for that: brokers racing a rival’s petition to the Canopy Court, factors with news from a ship, families with a deathbed at the far end of the realm. The runners are young, quick, well paid and soon old. The Cradle calls this *running green*, and parents in the strand villages say it the way parents elsewhere say *running off to sea*.

**The Daykeepers of the Cradle** are fewer than the Cradle needs and busier than any in the realm. They stamp the Ledgers at the strand-gates, weigh and stamp the harvest at the Threshing Mounds, witness every marriage and every planting, and give every child its day-name, which in the Cradle is a strange business: day-names come from the outside calendar, and six sleeps of babies are born in every outside day. **Everyone in the Cradle born in the same sun shares a day-name**, and a Daykeeper names them all at once, at the strand-gate, at dawn. The Cradle uses birth-names for everything but law, and has a great many proverbs about the dangers of being summoned to court by your day-name alone.

**Foreigners** hardly live in the Cradle at all, with one exception, and the Cradle wishes there were none. Every ageing contract written at Koneleth must name a **sitter**: someone of the client’s own choosing who stays with the cargo through the whole term, in the district where it is aged, to witness that what comes out is what went in. The Cradle will provide sitters cheaply. The Crownweave trading houses do not trust them, and hire their own instead, from the docks and debtors’ rolls of Stormwake and the Crown: men and women who will sit with a cellar of brandy in the Cradle for two outside years, which is twelve of their own, for a sum of Crownsblood that would take them twenty to earn at home. The Cradle calls them **cask-sitters**. Most go home rich and middle-aged. Some marry into a terrace-line and do not go home. A few were not told, when they signed, what *lived year* meant.

The Balam keep the Cradle’s outer edge from their posts in the eastern boughs, at the drum-tower of Lak’inir and the villages of Juulir and Mu’ukir, and rarely come down onto the terraces. The Cradle keeps its own peace, mostly by talking.

### **III. Of Grain, Tithe, and the Price of Haste**

Iximvael is **Thal’vireth’s granary**, and a granary is judged by what leaves it.

The Cradle grows, in the old way, maize, beans and squash on the same terrace, the maize for the beans to climb and the squash to shade the roots, and calls a terrace planted so a *kol*. Around the kol it grows nearly everything else the realm eats:

* maize of four colours, white, yellow, red and blue, each with its own terraces and its own bread;
* beans, squash and chili;
* cacao, on the shaded upper terraces, which the whole realm drinks and the Cradle grows almost alone;
* honey from the stingless forest bees of the orchard-groves;
* fruit, from avocado and guava to the orchard-fruits the outside world has no names for;
* cotton and dye-plants for the realm’s bark-cloth and textiles;
* herbs and simples for the Quickening Wards’ tinctures;
* vanilla, grown on vines on the southern slopes, which the Wards buy for their perfumes and Crownweave buys for its cakes;
* and marigolds, by the field, for the plantings of the whole realm.

It harvests four and five times in every outside year, by the Road’s count, and once or twice a lived year by its own, and every harvest goes three ways.

**The Tithe** goes up. A fixed share of every harvest is weighed at the Threshing Mounds, stamped, and carted up the Root-Road to the Canopy Court and the Deep Count, which between them grow nothing at all. Because the Deep Count keeps so slow a pace, a cart of Cradle grain that reaches Tun’iel feeds the archive for a very long time. The Heartwood Stacks are, at this moment, still eating bread baked from a harvest whose growers have all been planted, and whose growers’ grandchildren are being planted now. The Cradle finds this funnier than the Stacks do.

**The Granary Road** goes down. The rest of the harvest, less what the Cradle eats, goes on the Granary carts to the sea: to the Road’s inns, to Nabireth’s ships, and to Crownweave, which buys Cradle maize and cacao by the hold and has never been told that the farmers who grew it were born after the ship that carries it set sail.

**The cellars** take what comes in. The Cradle is one of two districts that take ageing contracts, and the one with the most room. Wine, brandy and spirits, timber to be seasoned, cheese, tobacco and cures come up the Road from Koneleth, go down the Strands into the Cradle’s long cellars, and come out, an outside year or two later, six or twelve years older. The Cradle’s share of the price is fixed at Koneleth, in jade-tally, by the Curia’s schedule. It is not large.

***The Price of Haste.*** The Cradle sells at the Road’s prices and buys at them, and the Road sets its prices on true time. The rates on the board at Koneleth change once an outside season. Inside the Cradle a season is a year and a half, and in a year and a half a great deal can happen to the price of maize, none of which the board will admit until it next changes. In a good year the Cradle sells a glut at a dearth’s price and does well. In a bad one it sells a dearth at a glut’s, and the terraces go hungry for a year that the Road calls three months. The Cradle has a word for the gap between what the board says and what the hillside knows, which the Compendium has been asked not to print, and a saying that it has been encouraged to: *the Road is always last year*.

### **IV. Law and the Long Petition**

The Cradle is governed, like every district, by its **Ah K’in**, who holds the bond that lets them read and set its pace, and by the Daykeepers beneath them. In the Cradle, the arrangement has one difficulty that no other district shares. Nobody who lives in the Cradle can hold the office long enough to learn it.

The Curia has therefore always chosen the Cradle’s Ah K’in from outside it, and the present Ah K’in of the Cradle, **Tunsael**, at law *Bolon Ajaw*, is an elf of the Deep Count who keeps his seat in the Canopy Court and comes down to the Cradle one sun in each outside season to hear petitions. He hears them at Kolvael, on **the Keeper’s Stair** (see below), which is kept at true time for the purpose. He has held the bond for some forty outside years, which in the Cradle is two hundred and forty; he is gentle, scrupulous, well read in the Cradle’s customs and quite genuinely fond of its people, and he has never once in his life been in a hurry. He is the oldest thing that most Cradle families have ever seen that is not a tree. Grandmothers on the Hundred Terraces remember their grandmothers’ stories of him, and point out that he looks the same. The Cradle’s view of him is summed up in its saying that *the Keeper comes every year and a half, and stays a week*.

Below him the Cradle’s **Daykeepers** keep its Ledgers and its stamps, and its **terrace moots** keep almost everything else. The moots settle boundaries, water-rights, cistern-shares and marriages, and send up to the Keeper only what they cannot settle, which they regard as a confession of failure. The Balam seldom come down onto the terraces, and the moots prefer it that way.

In the Cradle:

* **Sentences** are given, as everywhere in the realm, in lived years. A Cradle farmer sentenced to ten lived years of labour on the Root-Road serves them in an outside year and a half, if served in the Cradle, and in ten if sent up the Stone. Which one a sentence is to be served in is the most argued-over line in any Cradle judgment.
* **Moving a terrace wall**, even by a hand’s breadth, is the Cradle’s oldest crime and the one its moots punish hardest.
* **False weight at the Mounds**, and false stamps on the tithe, are crimes against the Curia, and the Curia tries them itself, eventually.
* **Running a strand**, crossing between the Road and the Cradle anywhere but through a strand-gate, is not a crime in itself, as nowhere in the realm is; it merely leaves the runner with days that no court will agree they lived. The Cradle-runners, who do it constantly, take the view that they will not be needing them.

***The Full Measure.*** For as long as anyone in the Cradle can remember, which is not, by the Road’s count, very long, the Cradle’s highest honour has been to *feed the Count*: to work the terraces sixty lived years, send the tithe up the hill, be planted in the Field, and have your name cut into a wall and your bead-cord hung beside it, and nothing more. The Cradle’s elders call this the humble life, and mean it as the highest praise they have. Lately, on the terraces, there has been another kind of talk, which the elders call *the proud talk*, and which its own speakers call **the Full Measure**: that a district which lives six times as fast should have six times the say, or at the least a Keeper of its own blood and a seat in the Canopy Court; that the tithe should be measured in lived years, *a measure of say for a measure of grain*; and, from its louder speakers, that if the Canopy Court will not hear it, the Cradle might simply hold back one harvest’s tithe and see how long the Still can wait.

The loudest of those speakers is **Kolaya**, at law *Lajun K’an*, a day-name she shares with some three hundred others born in her sun. She is head of one of the oldest lines on the Hundred Terraces, whose name has been cut into its wall eleven times. She speaks well, at every moot within a day’s walk, and she has never once climbed the Keeper’s Stair: *I will not spend my years at the foot of a stair*, she says, *to ask a man who has never spent one for the price of my bread.* She has been saying so for nine lived years, which in the Canopy Court is a season and a half, and the Court has not yet entered it in the record. The Full Measure’s letters are carried by Cradle-runners, who are expensive, and its moots are lit through the long nights by glyph-lamps, which are dearer, and somebody has been paying for both. Nobody in the Cradle has asked who. Nobody in the Cradle has the time.

### **V. Certain Places of Note**

#### **1\. The Hundred Terraces**

*Where the Cradle writes its names.*

North of the White Road, three days up the Root-Road from Weneleth and a few days out from the Tower, the land rises into a great bowl of hillside, and the hillside has been cut, from the valley floor to the crest, into steps. These are **the Hundred Terraces**, at Kolvael, the largest and oldest field-works in the realm. There are, in fact, a hundred and sixty-two of them; the name was given when there were a hundred, before the Compact, and the Cradle has never seen the need to change it.

Each terrace is held by one line, and on its retaining wall, at the head of the stair that climbs past it, the line cuts the name of each new head of the household in turn. The oldest walls are cut so deep with names, a hand’s span below the last, row on row, that the stone has been replaced twice, and the old stones, with their names, built into the base of the new. A line that dies out has its last name cut and its terrace given to the line beside it, and its wall is left as it is.

When a Cradle farmer has worked their full sixty lived years and is planted, their cord of Count-beads is not planted with them. It is hung on a peg beside their name on the terrace wall, six or seven beads of green on a cotton cord, and left there until the cord rots and the beads fall into the terrace. The Cradle says the terrace has eaten them. Children of the Hundred Terraces are sent out after every Long Wet to pick fallen beads out of the mud, and keep them, and the oldest families have jars of them.

---

#### **2\. The Keeper’s Stair**

*Where the Cradle stops, for an hour, to be heard.*

At the foot of the Hundred Terraces, on the Root-Road, stands a short broad stair of pale stone, nine steps high, the same stone as the White Road and warded the same way, which climbs to a small open platform under a roof of grown wood. It is the only ground in the Cradle that keeps true time. It is called **the Keeper’s Stair**, and it is where the Ah K’in of the Cradle hears petitions on the one sun in each season that he comes down to hear them.

A petitioner on the Stair speaks at true time and is heard at true time. A petitioner at the foot of it, waiting their turn, is in the Cradle, and waits at the Cradle’s pace, and a queue that moves an outside hour’s worth costs every soul in it six of their own. The Cradle has dealt with this, in the way it deals with most things, by sending its children. A household with business before the Keeper sends a child of ten or so to stand in the queue with the papers, and the child is often old enough to speak for the household by the time it is called. The Keeper is understood to have noticed this, and to find it touching.

---

#### **3\. The Threshing Mounds**

*Where the harvest is weighed, and the tithe taken.*

Below the southern terraces at **Chaakvael**, where the Root-Road’s southern spur ends and the Granary Road begins, the land is flat for a mile, and on the flat stand the **Threshing Mounds**: long platforms of beaten earth, waist-high and faced with stone, on which the southern Cradle’s harvest is threshed, winnowed, weighed and stamped. In a harvest sun the Mounds are the loudest place in the realm, with a thousand flails going at once under the lamps and the chaff blowing off the platforms in drifts like snow.

At the end of each Mound sits a Daykeeper with a beam-scale and a stamp. Every basket is weighed, its weight entered in the harvest ledger, and its tithe-share measured off into the **tithe-carts**, which go up the Root-Road to Weneleth and the Canopy Court; the rest is stamped for the Granary carts and the sea. The Mound Daykeepers are the most watched officials in the Cradle, by the farmers below them and by the Curia above, and the Cradle has an old rhyme about them which it sings at the Mounds while they work, and which they pretend not to hear.

Chaakvael means *rain-field*, and the Mounds are also where the Cradle calls for rain. At the end of each Long Dry, when the cisterns are scraped and the terraces cracked, the moots of the southern Cradle gather on the Mounds through a whole sun with drums and frog-rattles and call the rains in. They have always come. The Daykeepers point out that they would have anyway. The Cradle points out that the Daykeepers have never tried not calling.

---

#### **4\. The Planting Field**

*Where the Cradle feeds the tree.*

Below the Hundred Terraces, a long surface root of Ix’kaanil, as thick as a house is tall, comes up out of the hillside and runs south along the valley floor for the better part of a day’s walk, going down again at last beside K’anvael. The ground on either side of it is **the Planting Field**, the Cradle’s root-bed, and the busiest in the realm.

The Cradle’s dead are planted here, as the realm’s dead are planted at every root-bed of the tree: laid in the open soil among the surface roots, without a stone or a name, and left for the tree to take. Because the Cradle lives fast, it dies often, and there is a planting in the Field nearly every sleep. Cradle plantings are short and cheerful. The mourners come in their best clothes, with marigolds, and stay to eat; there is music if the dead liked music and arguing if they liked arguing; and when it is over the Field is left to the children, who play on the great root and in the marigolds all through the long day, and are not stopped.

The first ear cut from every terrace at every harvest is brought here and planted with whoever is being planted that sleep, so that the tree, as the Cradle says, eats first. The Field is very green.

---

#### **5\. The Long Cellars of Ki’vael**

*Where the outside world comes to grow old quickly.*

A day north of the Road, at the head of the Strand from Chi’eleth, the village of **Ki’vael**, the sweet field, stands on a hill that is mostly hollow. The hill has been tunnelled into cellars, and the cellars run back into the rock in long, cool, lamp-lit galleries racked to the roof with casks, crates and bales under the seals of a dozen Crownweave houses. These are the **Long Cellars**, the largest ageing-house in the realm, where most of the contracts written at Koneleth for the Cradle are kept.

Every rack in the Long Cellars has its sitter, and every sitter lives at Ki’vael, in rows of small houses that the village rents to them by the sun. Cask-sitters keep to themselves, mostly, play cards, write letters home, and walk the galleries every sleep to count their casks and check the seals; a good many take up a trade in the village to pass the years, and a few have taken up farming. The village is fond of them in the way a village is fond of anyone who spends money and does not stay long. Every so often one of them stays.

The Cellars’ keeper, a Cradle elf of the Ki’vael terrace-lines, keeps a board in the entrance-gallery on which each sitter’s name is chalked with the day their contract ends, in outside time, and beside it, in a second column, their lived age on that day. Most sitters stop reading the second column within a year.

---

#### **6\. The Honey-Groves of Kabvael**

*Where the bees are always swarming.*

On the Granary Road east of the Threshing Mounds, among the orchard-groves of the lower Cradle, **Kabvael** keeps the realm’s bees. They are the stingless bees of the western forest, small and black and gentle, and the Cradle keeps them in the old way, in hollow logs hung in rows under thatched sheds, the ends stopped with clay and a hole bored for the bees to come and go.

At the Cradle’s pace a hive grows, splits and swarms many times in an outside year, and Kabvael’s honey-keepers spend their lives catching swarms out of the orchard trees and boxing them into new logs; the sheds of Kabvael run for miles. Its honey, thin, sour-sweet and faintly floral, is laid down in sealed jars in the village’s own cellars and comes out in an outside season as dark and thick as old wine. Kabvael honey is a medicine in the Wards, a luxury on the Road, and a gift fit for an Ah K’in, and the honey-keepers say the bees are the only creatures in the Cradle older than they look, which nobody has been able to prove or disprove.

---

#### **7\. The Runners’ Way**

*The fastest road in the realm, and the most expensive.*

No map that the Curia publishes shows **the Runners’ Way**, and every Cradle-runner can walk it in the dark. It leaves the White Road by the northern strand-gate at Kaajeleth, two days out of Nabireth, runs north-west across the Cradle by Keejvael, Kumvael, Kakavael, Chelvael and Najvael, and comes out at Wayebiel in the Deep Count, beside the Waiting Wards: eight days’ walk by the runner’s count, and not much over one by anyone else’s. A traveller who keeps to the stone, from Kaajeleth up the Road to Weneleth and up the Root-Road to Wayebiel, takes nine. From Wayebiel the Root-Road runs on to Tun’iel and the Tower.

It is used for one purpose: to get something from the sea to the Tower faster than the White Road can. The Exchange Houses use it to put a broker’s petition into the Canopy Court’s queue before a rival’s. The families of the dying in the Waiting Wards use it to carry the news that a cure has been found. Somebody, the Compendium is told, uses it to carry things that would not pass the Counting Pier. The Daykeeper at Kaajeleth’s strand-gate stamps the runners’ Ledgers when they set out and the Daykeepers of Wayebiel stamp them when they arrive, and between the two stamps a runner has lived the best part of a week for a fee that would keep a Road family for a season. Runners who have run the Way a hundred times are rich, and look it, and look a good deal else besides.

### **VI. The Terraces and the Strands**

*A gazetteer of the district, by its four countries. Distances are given in days’ walk as the walker lives them; by the outside clock, divide by six.*

The Cradle divides, by the habit of those who farm it, into four countries:

* **The Upper Cradle** – the high terraces north of the Road, from the Root-Road east to Lak’inir, bordering the Deep Count.
* **The North Strands** – the villages hung off the Strands north of the Road.
* **The South Strands** – the villages hung off the Strands south of it.
* **The Granary Country** – the lower Cradle in the south, along the Granary Road and down to the Quickening Wards’ slopes.

The **Root-Road** runs north from Weneleth through the Upper Cradle to the Deep Count, and sends a spur south to the Threshing Mounds; the **Granary Road** runs from the Mounds east through the Granary Country to the White Road at Ookeleth; and the **Runners’ Way** crosses the whole Cradle from Kaajeleth to Wayebiel. The Strands leave the White Road through their strand-gates, which are described with the Road.

#### **The Upper Cradle**

**Kolvael** — *The Hundred Terraces.*  
The Cradle’s largest town and its heart, about nine thousand by the Ledger, on the Root-Road three days north of Weneleth and a day short of the Deep Count’s border at Wayebiel. Its houses stand on the terraces themselves, each line’s house at the head of its own stair, and its town-square is the uppermost terrace, where the moot of the Hundred Terraces meets. The Keeper’s Stair stands at the foot of the hill, and the Planting Field runs south from below it.

**K’anvael** — *The Yellow Field.*  
A village on the Root-Road a day south of Kolvael, at the southern end of the Planting Field, named for the yellow maize of its terraces. Its people dig the plantings, and sell marigolds to the mourners.

**Najvael** — *The House-Field.*  
A village on the Runners’ Way two days north-east of Kolvael, the last in the Cradle before the Deep Count, built around the long storehouses where the tithe for Tun’iel and the Waiting Wards is held before it goes up the Root-Road. The runners rest here, and so do the tithe-carters; the two do not drink together.

**Muchvael** — *The Frog-Field.*  
A hamlet a day east of Najvael, among wet terraces and ponds whose frogs sing all through the Long Wet. The Cradle says the rains are coming when Muchvael’s frogs begin, and sends to Muchvael for frog-rattles every time they are late.

**Chelvael** — *The Rainbow-Field.*  
A village on the Runners’ Way a day south of Muchvael, whose terraces grow the Cradle’s cotton and dye-plants in bands of colour down the hillside, and whose weavers make the realm’s finest bark-cloth and cotton. Quetzal-dyed thread is spun here for the Ah K’in, under seal.

**Luumvael** — *The Earth-Field.*  
A hamlet of potters a day north-east of Muchvael and a day and a half from the drum-tower at Lak’inir. It makes the Cradle’s water-jars, cistern-linings and the plain red bowls the whole realm eats from.

**Pek’vael** — *The Dog-Field.*  
A village a day south-east of Luumvael, which breeds the Cradle’s herding and guard dogs, small, hairless and clever, and sells them to the Balam, who use them to carry messages along the outer boughs.

#### **The North Strands**

**Ki’vael** — *The Sweet Field.*  
A village a day north of the Road, at the head of the oldest Strand on the Road, which leaves the stone through the strand-gate at Chi’eleth. The Long Cellars run under its hill, and its rows of rented houses are full of cask-sitters.

**Buulvael** — *The Bean-Field.*  
A village two and a half days north-east of the Road, at the end of the westernmost Strand, which leaves the stone just above Weneleth. The first Cradle village a traveller from the Tower reaches; its strand-gate is where most foreigners who see the Cradle at all go to look at it.

**Kumvael** — *The Squash-Field.*  
A village two days east of Buulvael along its Strand, among squash terraces whose fruit, in a good Long Wet, are too heavy for one man to lift.

**Kakavael** — *The Cacao-Field.*  
A hamlet two days north-east of Buulvael, on shaded terraces under tall forest trees, where most of the realm’s cacao is grown, fermented and dried. Its cacao is sold by the sack at Koneleth and by the cup everywhere else.

**Kuxvael** — *The Living Field.*  
A village a day north of the Road, on a Strand that leaves the stone a little below Chi’eleth, which keeps the Cradle’s seed: every strain of maize, bean and squash the Cradle has ever grown, planted out on its terraces a few rows at a time so that none is lost. The best of its seed goes up the Road to be kept in the Deep Count under a keeping contract, against the day the Cradle needs it back.

**Keejvael** — *The Deer-Field.*  
A village two days north-east of the Road on a long Strand, where the terraces end against the eastern boughs and the forest deer come down at night to raid them. It is the first stop on the Runners’ Way, and its trails run on to the Balam villages of Juulir and Mu’ukir.

**Chanvael** — *The Little Field.*  
A village under the eastern boughs, three days south of Pek’vael, and larger than Nojvael, *the great field*, in the south. The two villages were founded, the story goes, by twin sisters who quarrelled over which had the better land and named their villages to settle it. Nobody remembers which won.

#### **The South Strands**

**Nikvael** — *The Flower-Field.*  
A village two days south of the Road, on a Strand from the stone between Weneleth and Chi’eleth, whose terraces are planted with marigolds from the valley floor to the crest. It supplies the plantings of the whole realm, and in a harvest sun the whole hillside is orange.

**Xiwvael** — *The Herb-Field.*  
A village a day and a half south of Nikvael, whose terraces grow herbs and simples for the Quickening Wards’ tinctures and medicines. Its herb-women sell to the Wards by the sack and to the Cradle by the pinch, and know which is the better bargain.

**Yaxvael** — *The Green Field.*  
A hamlet on a Strand a day east of Ookeleth, on the Cradle’s eastern edge. In the last outside year a terrace of Yaxvael maize came up white to the root, ear and leaf and silk, and the Daykeepers had it burned before the Compendium’s correspondent could see it. The village says the water did it. Its water comes down from the carters’ yards at Ookeleth, where Nabireth’s cargo waits for oxen.

#### **The Granary Country**

**Chaakvael** — *The Threshing Mounds.*  
A town of about six thousand, three and a half days south of Weneleth at the end of the Root-Road’s southern spur, where the southern Cradle’s harvest is threshed, weighed and stamped, and where the Granary Road begins. The Mounds lie on the flat below the town, the Daykeepers’ harvest-hall beside them, and the tithe-carts and Granary carts leave from either end.

**Kabvael** — *The Honey-Groves.*  
An orchard village a day and a half east of Chaakvael on the Granary Road, with the realm’s bee-sheds in its groves and its honey-cellars under them.

**Lolvael** — *The Blossom-Field.*  
A hamlet a day beyond Kabvael on the Granary Road, among the orchards, where the Granary carts water their oxen before the last stretch to Ookeleth. In blossom-time its groves are white, then pink, then fruit, then white again, within a single outside month.

**Ujvael** — *The Moon-Field.*  
A hamlet a day south-east of Xiwvael, whose people work their terraces only at night, by the moon and by lamps, and sleep through the light. They say it is cooler. The rest of the Cradle says they are odd.

**Ak’vael** — *The Vine-Field.*  
A village on the southern slopes, a day and a half south-west of Ujvael, where vanilla vines are grown on the trunks of the orchard trees and the pods cured in the sun on long racks. Its trail runs on to Saasarel in the Quickening Wards.

**Nojvael** — *The Great Field.*  
A village on the Cradle’s south-eastern edge, a day west of the Balam hamlet of Ik’ir, smaller than Chanvael, *the little field*, in the north. See Chanvael.

### **VII. Advice to the Traveller**

*A marginal note, copied from the back of a cask-sitter’s contract that was found at Ki’vael. The hand grows steadier towards the bottom of the page, and then less steady.*

* “Count your sleeps, not your suns. The sun here is a liar.”

* “Get your Ledger stamped at the gate even if you only meant to look at the terraces. Especially then.”

* “Eat when they feed you. Say ‘the tree eats first’ before you do. Do not reach for the bread before the eldest at the table, even if the eldest is younger than you.”

* “Do not promise anybody anything for ‘tomorrow’. Ask them which tomorrow they mean.”

* “Write home every sleep. Your letters will reach the Road in bundles of six, and your mother will think you have gone mad. Write anyway.”

* “Do not go to a planting dressed for a funeral. Bring marigolds. Stay and eat.”

* “If you have business with the Keeper, send a child to the Stair. By the time you are called, it will be old enough to speak for you.”

* “Read the second column on the board. Read it every sleep.”

*Thus ends the entry on Iximvael and the Green Cradle. For the White Road, whose Strands reach its villages, see the entry that precedes it; for the Quickening Wards, on whose slopes the Granary Country ends, see the entries that follow.*
