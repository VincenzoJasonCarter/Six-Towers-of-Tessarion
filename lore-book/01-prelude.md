# Prelude

Long before coin and contract ruled the roads, the world knew only towers.

No one agrees who raised them. Some say the gods hammered them up from the bones of the earth. Others whisper they grew on their own, like stone teeth pushing through the skin of reality. What *is* known is this: when the first people opened their eyes, the Six Towers were already there, casting long shadows over the newborn world.

At the center stood the **Meridian Spire**, where humans eventually gathered. They did not have the elves’ grace, nor the orcs’ strength, nor the dwarves’ craft, nor the tieflings’ dangerous charm, nor the Sangrith’s terrible hunger. What they had instead was restlessness—and an uncanny knack for talking to everyone without being entirely loved or entirely hated. Around the base of that central tower rose a crossroads of trade and rumor, the great city now called **Hexaspire**, heart of the world of **Tessarion**.

Around Hexaspire, like a tightening ring, the other peoples claimed their domains.

To the green, whispering east, the **elves of Thal’vireth** wrapped their forests around the roots of the **Verdant Tower**, a spire swallowed by living wood and pale stone. They speak of memory like it is a weapon, and time like it is a road only they remember how to walk.

To the harsh, ragged south, the **Iron Hordes of the orcs** raised war-camps in the shadow of the **Red Tower**, its stone stained like dried blood. They laugh loud, shout louder, and carve their history into scars and steel.

To the jagged north, beneath mountains that never thaw, the **dwarves of Khazrund** tunneled under the **Deep Tower**, a column of black rock veined with gleaming ore. Their cities are stacked downward instead of upward; their prayers are spoken in the language of hammers.

To the west, where the land is cracked and kissed by ember-winds, the **tieflings of Emberfall** built tiered cities around the **Ashen Tower**. Here, deals are struck in whispers and ink, and truth is only one of many acceptable currencies.

And high above them all, where clouds scrape exposed stone and even dragons grow wary, stands the **Obsidian Crown**—the sixth and highest tower. There dwells the **Sangrith**, the pale, patient apex of Tessarion. They are few in number and vast in influence, their long fingers sunk into every war and every market, feeding on the quiet arithmetic of blood.

War came early.

The first battles were simple: border skirmishes, insults repaid with raid. But hatred is a craftsman; it refines itself. In time, each person found in another a perfect enemy. Elves bled orcs beneath broken boughs. Orcs burned elf groves in reply. Dwarves cursed the infernal taint of tieflings; tieflings smiled and wrote new contracts in dwarven blood. Old grudges hardened into law and story.

From that endless killing, the Sangrith learned something no other race had the stomach—or the imagination—for.

They discovered that where the dead lay thick, the ground remembered. Soul-stuff seeped downward, pooling in silent strata. Given enough time and pressure, those layers of memory hardened into crystals: glowing veins of **mana**, each shard colored by the people who died above it. Verdant green beneath elven woods. Burning crimson under orc battlefields. Deep amber in dwarven halls. Sickly violet in tiefling ashlands.

The Sangrith called it **soulstone**, and they learned to cut it, drink it, weaponize it.

The rest of Tessarion only noticed when the crystals began to surface—when human miners beneath the Meridian Spire struck a vein of faintly glowing stone and didn’t die touching it. Instead, they changed. Their eyes brightened, their thoughts sharpened, and strange energies bled from their fingers. Where elves and dwarves burned or sickened from prolonged exposure, **humans adapted**. They could take in this crystallized soul-magic and survive. With training, they could wield it.

That was when humans stopped being dismissed as “clever, fragile middle-folk” and started being called something else:

**mages.**

In elven lands, human children who spent too long near green soulstone began to twist the forest with a thought, warping roots and bending light. In orcish badlands, those raised among red crystals learned to shatter stone and bone with explosive force. Among dwarves, amber-lit caverns bred humans who could raise shimmering wards that outlasted steel. In tiefling cities, violet facets taught humans to bind shadows and lay curses with a word. And those few unfortunate—or ambitious—enough to touch the Sangrith’s prismatic black crystal learned to tamper not just with mana, but with the soul itself.

The Sangrith watched this with cold interest. To them, humans had gone from amusing traders to something more complex: **tools that sharpen themselves.**

Meanwhile, another invention spread across Tessarion: **blood coin.**

If souls sank to make crystals, blood rose to stain metal. The Sangrith perfected the art first, fusing regional alloys with condensed blood from sworn enemies. The idea slithered downward like all dangerous innovations do. Soon, elven mints were hammering out pale silver coins streaked with orc blood. Orc forges spat black iron discs veined with elf red. Dwarves pressed heavy gold Crowns pulsing faintly with tiefling essence, and tieflings answered with dark brass Sins, each etched with runes fed by dwarven blood.

The apex race, of course, went further. In the vaults beneath the Obsidian Crown, the Sangrith melted star-iron with blood drawn from **all four** outer races, striking coins known as **Sovereigns**—currency that thrummed like a heartbeat when stacked, each piece a small, cold declaration of ownership over the world’s hate.

In the middle of it all, humans did what humans always do.

They built counting houses in the shadow of the Meridian Spire. They learned to weigh elf Oathleaves against orc Thorns, to tally dwarf Crowns against tiefling Sins. They carried coin from warfront to warfront, then wrote the first bloodless scripts: paper promises backed by vaults of metal and blood. At the same time, they tunneled deeper, chasing veins of soulstone, training new crystal mages to defend the caravans and the cities that grew fat from trade.

Now, the Six Towers still stand. The ring of races is still unsteady, bound together by grudges, coins, and the quiet glow of crystals under their feet. Humans walk the roads between them, pockets full of other peoples’ hatred and hands stained with the light of dead souls.

In the taverns of Hexaspire, old storytellers say Tessarion is holding its breath.

Sovereigns are appearing more often in the city’s markets. Strange anomalies follow in their wake—coins that whisper in the night, crystals that flicker with unfamiliar colors, mages whose powers blur the lines between path and path.

Some claim the towers are beginning to wake.

Others think Tessarion has simply reached the bill it cannot pay.

Either way, if there is going to be a story of change in this world, it will start—like all things do—at the center, with humans standing between five hungry powers, and mana crystals slowly drinking the souls of the fallen.
